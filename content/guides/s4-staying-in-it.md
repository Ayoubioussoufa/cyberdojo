Two years is long. Most people who start a self-taught path don't finish it, and it's rarely because the material was too hard. They stop because motivation runs out, life gets busy, or progress seems to stall. This guide is about the long game.

## Practice that improves you

Anders Ericsson studied how experts get good and described **deliberate practice**: practice designed to improve one specific weakness, at the edge of your ability, with immediate feedback ([Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer, 1993](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.100.3.363)). Ten hours of solving easy boxes you already know how to solve is mostly repetition. One hour on the thing you keep failing is practice.

The research also needs a caveat. A later meta-analysis found that deliberate practice explains a large share of the differences between people in games and music, but much less in education and in professions ([Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614535810)). Practice matters a great deal; it isn't everything. Good material, good feedback, rest and some luck matter too. So you can stop believing that anyone who's ahead of you simply worked more hours.

What deliberate practice looks like here:

- **Pick the weak spot on purpose.** Your [Progress](/skills/) page and [your mistakes](/practice/mistakes/) show it.
- **Get feedback fast.** Graded terminal missions, case files, and the "Copy for my AI tutor" button on written answers.
- **Stay at the edge.** If everything is right, move up a level. If almost everything is wrong, step down.

@term deliberate practice = Focused practice on a specific weakness, just beyond current ability, with fast feedback.

?? You've solved twenty easy rooms this month and feel stuck. What's the best next step?
+ Target the one technique you keep failing at
- Solve twenty more easy rooms to build speed
- Take a month off until the motivation returns
- Switch to a completely different field of security
=> Deliberate practice targets a specific weakness at the edge of your ability. More of what you can already do feels productive, but it mostly maintains your level.

## Plateaus are normal

Progress isn't a straight line. You'll spend weeks feeling no better, and then several things click at once. Usually the plateau is when the pieces are being connected underneath.

Three ways to measure progress that don't lie:

1. **Redo something from three months ago.** Time it. The difference is your progress.
2. **Read an old write-up of yours.** If it now embarrasses you, you've improved.
3. **Explain a topic to a beginner.** Notice how much you can now say without notes.

## Sleep, exercise and attention

- **Sleep consolidates memory.** During sleep the brain replays and stabilises what you learned during the day ([Rasch & Born, 2013](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.00032.2012)). A late night of studying often loses more than it gains.
- **Protect attention.** Phone in another room, one tab, one task. A short session with full attention beats a long one with notifications.
- **Move.** A walk between study blocks is a cheap reset, and many people find their best ideas there.

::: trap Cramming before an exam
Staying up late to cram the night before trades tomorrow's sleep, which is when today's learning gets consolidated, for a little extra short-term recall. Over two years, steady spaced study beats every all-nighter.
:::

## Burnout and a sustainable pace

Security can feel endless: there's always another tool, another CVE, another course. That's true for professionals too, and it's why the good ones choose what to ignore.

- **Plan the hours you can keep in a bad week, not a good one.** The [weekly schedules guide](/guides/i1-weekly-schedules/) helps you pick.
- **Keep a minimum day.** On a bad day, do only [Review](/review/) and nothing else. The streak survives, and so does the habit.
- **Take real breaks.** A planned week off every few months is part of the plan, not a failure of it.
- **Compare with yourself three months ago, not with strangers online.** People post their wins, not their 40 failed attempts.
- **If studying makes you anxious most days**, talk to someone: a friend, a mentor, or your university's student support service. That's not a weakness. It's maintenance.

?? Your week is chaotic and you've no energy. What keeps the habit alive?
+ Do only today's short review, then stop there
- Skip the week, then double up on Sunday
- Force a full four-hour session despite the fatigue
- Delete your progress and restart when life's calmer
=> A minimum day keeps the habit and the spacing schedule going at almost no cost. Doubling up later is cramming, and skipping often turns into quitting.

## Communities: where knowledge becomes wisdom

Courses give knowledge. Labs give skills. Judgement comes from other people: what matters in a real job, which advice is outdated, how an interview really goes. Join at least one community and take part, even quietly at first.

- **Local meetups.** [OWASP chapters](https://owasp.org/chapters/) meet in many cities, often for free, and [BSides](https://www.securitybsides.com/) conferences are cheap, friendly and community-run.
- **Online.** [r/netsecstudents](https://www.reddit.com/r/netsecstudents/) for learners, and [r/AskNetsec](https://www.reddit.com/r/AskNetsec/) for questions to practitioners.
- **Platforms' own communities.** The forums and chat servers of the [platforms you use](/guides/e1-platforms/).
- **Your university.** Many have a security club or CTF team. If yours doesn't, starting one is a strong line on a CV.

How to take part well: read for a while, ask questions that show what you tried, answer beginners' questions when you can (that's teaching, and teaching is learning), and share your write-ups.

?! Name one community you'll join this week. Write the first question you'd ask there, using the format "what I tried, the exact error, what I expected".
