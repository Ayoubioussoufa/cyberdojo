You'll spend two years on this course. Whether you still know the material at the end depends far less on talent than on *how* you study. The research on learning is unusually clear, and it's often the opposite of what feels right. This guide covers the findings that matter, cites the studies behind them, and shows where each one is built into CyberDojo.

## Two kinds of strength

Robert and Elizabeth Bjork, who run a learning and forgetting lab at UCLA, describe every memory as having two separate strengths ([Bjork Learning and Forgetting Lab](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/research/)):

- **Retrieval strength**: how easily you can bring it to mind *right now*.
- **Storage strength**: how deeply it's learned, and so how long it will last.

They don't move together. Reread a page about Kerberos and retrieval strength shoots up: it all looks familiar, and you feel you know it. Storage strength barely moves. A week later the feeling has gone, and so has the knowledge.

The Bjorks' key point is that conditions which make practice *feel harder* often build more storage strength. They call these **desirable difficulties**. Struggling to recall a port number, mixing topics, waiting a few days before reviewing: each feels worse in the moment, and each makes learning last longer.

::: key The rule this guide keeps returning to
How easy studying *feels* is a bad measure of how much you're *learning*. Easy, fluent study is often wasted. Effortful study that you get partly wrong is often the most productive hour of your week.
:::

@term retrieval strength = How easily a memory can be recalled right now. It rises fast and fades fast.
@term storage strength = How deeply a memory is learned, which decides how long it lasts.
@term desirable difficulty = A condition that makes practice harder in the moment but improves long-term learning.

?? You reread your notes on TLS and they all feel familiar. What has most likely happened?
+ Retrieval strength rose, but storage strength barely changed
- Storage strength rose, so the notes are permanent
- Both strengths rose by the same large amount
- Neither strength changed, because you only read silently
=> Familiarity is retrieval strength: it rises quickly and fades quickly. Rereading builds very little storage strength, which is why the knowledge is gone a week later.

## The forgetting curve

In 1885 Hermann Ebbinghaus memorised lists of nonsense syllables and tested himself after different delays. He found that most forgetting happens quickly, and then slows down ([Ebbinghaus, *Memory*, English translation](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Ebbinghaus/index.htm)). In 2015 Murre and Dros repeated his experiment and got almost the same curve, with one interesting bump: recall after 24 hours was slightly *better* than expected, which they linked to the night's sleep ([Murre & Dros, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0120644)).

Two practical points follow:

1. **Without review, most of a lesson is lost within days.** That's normal, not a sign you're bad at this.
2. **Each review flattens the curve.** Something recalled successfully after a gap fades more slowly the next time.

@term forgetting curve = The rapid then slowing loss of memory over time, first measured by Ebbinghaus in 1885.

## Retrieval practice: test yourself to learn

The most robust finding in this whole field is that *recalling* information strengthens it far more than *re-reading* it.

- Roediger and Karpicke had students either reread a passage or take recall tests on it. After five minutes, rereading looked better. After a week, the students who had been tested remembered clearly more, even though the rereading group *predicted* they would do better ([Roediger & Karpicke, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x)).
- Karpicke and Blunt compared retrieval practice with concept mapping, a well-regarded "deep" study method. Retrieval practice won, even when the final test asked students to *draw a concept map* ([Karpicke & Blunt, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1199327)).
- A major review of ten common study techniques rated practice testing as one of only two with "high utility" ([Dunlosky et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)).

A test is a tool for learning, not just for measuring it. That's why every lesson here has questions in every section, and why you should answer before scrolling on.

::: try Do it now
Close this page. On paper, write the two kinds of memory strength and one sentence on each. Then come back and check. That small, slightly uncomfortable effort did more for your memory than reading the section twice.
:::

@term retrieval practice = Learning by pulling information out of memory, for example with self-tests or flashcards, instead of rereading.

?? Which study session builds the longest-lasting memory of the TCP handshake?
+ Draw the handshake from memory, then check it
- Reread the TCP section slowly, two more times
- Copy the handshake diagram neatly into your notebook
- Highlight every flag name in the lesson yellow
=> Drawing it from memory is retrieval practice. Rereading, copying and highlighting keep the material in front of you, so you never have to retrieve it.

## Spacing: the same hours, spread out

Studying something in several sessions spread over days beats the same total time in one block. A review of hundreds of experiments found this **spacing effect** again and again ([Cepeda et al., 2006](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354)). A later large study found that the best gap between sessions grows with how long you need to remember: for a test next week, a gap of about a day works well; to remember something for a year, gaps of several weeks work better ([Cepeda et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x)).

You need this for two years, so you want growing gaps: a day, then a few days, then a week, then a month. That's what the [Review page](/review/) does with every question you answer, and what [Anki](https://docs.ankiweb.net/) does with flashcards.

Cramming isn't useless: it can get you through tomorrow's exam. It just leaves almost nothing behind a month later.

@term spacing effect = The finding that study spread across several sessions is remembered longer than the same study in one block.

## Interleaving: mix the problems

**Blocked** practice means ten subnetting questions, then ten port questions. **Interleaved** practice means the same questions shuffled together. In one study, students who practised maths problems shuffled scored far higher on a later test than students who practised them in blocks ([Rohrer & Taylor, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-007-9015-8)). In another, people learned to recognise painters' styles better from mixed examples, yet most of them *believed* blocked study had worked better ([Kornell & Bjork, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02127.x)).

Why does it work? When problems are mixed, you have to decide *which kind of problem this is* before solving it. In a real incident nobody tells you "this is a DNS question", so the deciding is the skill you need.

That's the idea behind [Today's mix](/practice/mix/). Use it most days, even though it feels harder than a single-topic drill.

@term interleaving = Mixing different kinds of problems in one practice session, so you must choose the method as well as apply it.

?? Your exam covers subnetting, ports and log analysis. How should you practise?
+ Shuffle questions from all three topics each session
- Finish every subnetting question before moving to ports
- Practise only the topic that feels the weakest
- Save all practice questions for the night before
=> Interleaving makes you identify the type of problem, which is what the exam and the job both require. It feels slower, and it works better.

## Explain it, and generate it

Two more techniques rated well in the research are cheap to use:

- **Self-explanation.** Students who explained each step of a text to themselves understood it better than those who read it twice ([Chi et al., 1994](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1803_3)). When a lesson shows a command, say *why* each part is there before running it.
- **The generation effect.** Information you produce yourself is remembered better than information you only read ([Slamecka & Graf, 1978](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.4.6.592)). Predict a command's output before you press Enter. Guess what a log line means before reading the explanation.

@term self-explanation = Explaining to yourself why each step of a solution or text is the way it is.
@term generation effect = The finding that information you produce yourself is remembered better than information you only read.

## Struggle before the answer

Manu Kapur gave students hard problems *before* teaching them the method. They mostly failed, and then learned the method better than students taught first, especially on problems that needed real understanding ([Kapur, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1080/07370000802212669)). He called it **productive failure**.

This is why project walkthroughs here are off by default, and why the terminal offers a hint only after five minutes. A short, honest struggle prepares your mind to grab the answer when it comes. The limit is the word *short*: struggling for three hours with no progress is just frustration. See [Studying hands-on](/guides/s3-studying-hands-on/) for a time-box rule.

@term productive failure = Attempting a problem before being taught how to solve it, which can deepen learning even when the attempt fails.

## What doesn't work as well as it feels

- **Rereading and highlighting** were rated "low utility" by the 2013 review ([Dunlosky et al.](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)). They're not harmful; they're just weak for the time they take.
- **Learning styles.** The idea that you learn best when teaching matches your "visual" or "auditory" style has no good evidence behind it ([Pashler et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x)). Pick the format that suits the *content*: diagrams for protocols, a terminal for commands.
- **Watching videos on repeat.** Watching is reading in another form. Pause, predict, and do it yourself.

?? A friend says she's a "visual learner", so she only watches videos. What does the research suggest?
+ Matching teaching to a learning style lacks good evidence
- Visual learners really do learn best only from video
- Everyone has one style, which a quiz can identify
- Styles matter, but only for technical subjects like this
=> Pashler and colleagues found no adequate evidence that matching instruction to a learning style improves learning. What helps everyone is retrieval, spacing and doing.

## Sleep is part of studying

During sleep the brain replays and stabilises what you learned that day ([Rasch & Born, 2013](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.00032.2012)). Cutting sleep to study more often loses more than it gains. A good pattern: study, then sleep, then a quick retrieval session the next morning.
